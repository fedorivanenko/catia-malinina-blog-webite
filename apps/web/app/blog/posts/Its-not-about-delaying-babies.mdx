What if reproductive aging was optional? 

What if fertility wasn’t a deadline, but a choice? If we could extend reproductive health by decades, how would that reshape our lives?

Right now, that’s not the case. I’m turning 32 this year. 

That means I have three years before fertility decline begins. 

Eight before it becomes steep. 

Thirteen before natural conception is unlikely. 

These numbers vary from woman to woman, but the trend is the same: reproductive aging is faster and more irreversible than almost any other system in the body.

And because of that, I face a choice. A hard, immediate, high-stakes choice.

One path is to push forward. To become a modern renaissance woman, someone who has it all. A branding strategist. A scientist developing ovarian rejuvenation protocols.

The other is to stop. To shift focus entirely to preconception, pregnancy, and motherhood. To plan for the future while my body still gives me the best odds.

These two cannot coexist. Whatever I choose, I lose something. Time. Opportunity. An alternate version of my life. And that loss is real.

But what if we didn’t have to choose? What if we had 20 or 30 more fertile years? If we could live to 150, dedicating decades to deep work, exploration, and family, how would that reshape our lives?

I can see it now — ten years building brands and writing. Ten in the university and the lab, researching everything fertility-related. Ten more launching a biotech startup. Ten as a VC. 

And somewhere in between, breaks to have as many children as I want — without urgency, without compromise.

That would be the life. A full life, free from artificial limits. We are more than “one career + three kids + retirement at 65.”

This isn’t just a fantasy. Lifespan has nearly doubled in the last century, but fertility hasn’t caught up.

Scientists are already identifying the molecular drivers of ovarian aging. Stem cell therapies, metabolic interventions, and cellular rejuvenation research are underway. Reproductive longevity should be treated the same way we treat cardiovascular and cognitive health.

Why isn’t it?

This isn’t about delaying motherhood. It’s about expanding human potential. About having the ability to dedicate decades to a pursuit without penalty.

We already assume that living to 90 or 100 should come with independence, energy, and function. Why should fertility be any different?

Radical life extension is coming. The only question is: will fertility extension come with it?

Because I don’t want to live just one life. 

I want to live many—within the same body.
